Class 10 ends with a decision that looks bigger than it is. Choose a stream, people say, and you
have chosen a career, a salary and a city. Choose wrong and you have wasted two years. That
pressure is real, and it is also mostly inherited from a time when one route led to one job.

Here is a way to make the choice that does not require you to predict your own future.

## Start with what you actually do, not what you admire

Ask a fifteen-year-old what they want to be and the honest answers are often cricket, content
creator, or "something with computers". Those are admired things, not described ones. A useful
question is narrower: **what have you done for more than an hour where losing track of time was
possible?**

Write down three or four real instances — not categories, incidents. Organising a school event
counts. Debugging a friend's phone counts. Explaining a chapter to a classmate until they got it
counts. Arguing with an uncle at a family dinner counts more than you think it does.

Those incidents have a shape. Some are about objects and systems, some about people, some about
ideas, some about making things, some about persuading, some about order and records. That shape is
what a stream choice should follow — the subjects are a means, not an identity.

## What the three streams actually contain

Science (PCM or PCB) is the stream with the most defined gatekeeping. It is the route into
engineering, medicine, pharmacy, architecture, many design-engineering overlaps, and most
research. Its cost is that the two years are dense and examinable, and if you are not going through
a specific gate, much of that density is not needed for the job you end up doing.

Commerce is the stream most often chosen by default and most often misunderstood. It is not
"business studies". The core is accounting, economics and business law — bookkeeping, cost sheets,
demand curves, contracts. It leads into CA, CS, CMA, company jobs, banking, insurance, analytics and
law as well as family businesses. If you like argument about how things are paid for, it fits.

Humanities is the stream with the widest ceiling and the least linear floor. Political science,
history, psychology, economics, sociology, philosophy, a second language. It leads into civil
services, law, journalism, design, publishing, teaching, policy, development work, and — increasingly
— research and public health. Its real difficulty is that nobody hands you a gate; you have to build
the route.

A fourth option exists in fewer schools than it should: **the National Education Policy allows
subjects to be mixed.** A student can take Psychology with Physics, or Economics with History. If
your school offers that, it is worth asking about before accepting a rigid choice.

## Test every claim before you build on it

Most advice about streams arrives as a confident sentence with no source. Run it through three
questions:

1. **Who said this, and what do they get out of saying it?** A coaching brochure that says "only
   Science students get into management" is selling seats. A career counsellor with no financial
   link to any college is closer to neutral.
2. **Is this a rule or a habit?** Rules come from a university, a regulator or an exam authority and
   are written down in a document you can ask for. Habits sound identical and are not verifiable.
3. **What is the cheapest way to check?** Almost every "you must have Science for this" claim can be
   tested by opening the eligibility section of the exam's official information bulletin. Not an
   aggregator's page — the body that conducts the exam.

This is why [a career profile that flags its own uncertainty](/careers) is more useful than one that
is confident about everything. Any figure that decides a family's spending — fees, seats, cutoffs,
dates — should be read from a current official document, not from a conversation.

## Involve the people who will actually support you

In India a stream choice is rarely a personal decision; it is a household decision, and the
household is paying. Trying to win an argument with a parent usually makes the position harder, not
easier. What works better is a specific, checkable plan:

- "I want to check whether Psychology can be taken with Biology in our board, and what the
  eligibility for the courses I am interested in says. Can we look at it together this weekend?"
- Bring the official document. A parent who sees a rule in writing argues with the rule, not with
  you.
- Name the fallback before you need it. "If this does not work out after one year, this is the other
  route" is a sentence that lowers the temperature of every conversation about it.

## Leave the decision open, on purpose

The useful output of this choice is not a career. It is a set of subjects you can study without
hating them, plus one or two things to try this year so the next decision has evidence under it. A
student who spends two months on one real project — a short app, a write-up for the school magazine,
volunteering at a local clinic, shadowing an electrician for a week — has more information than one
who spent two months worrying.

Nothing here is a prediction. Nobody can tell a fifteen-year-old what they will want at
twenty-two, and a tool that claims to is selling something. What a good process gives you is a
decision you can defend, and an obvious way to change it later.
