Every entrance exam season produces the same damage, and almost none of it comes from the exam. It
comes from families acting on a screenshot: a deadline that was actually last year's, an eligibility
rule that was actually a coaching centre's summary, a fee that was actually for a different
category. The document that decides all of this is public, and it is short enough to read. Most
people never do.

Here is how to read one.

## Find the body that conducts the exam, not the page that describes it

India's major exams are run by identifiable authorities — the National Testing Agency conducts
several undergraduate and postgraduate entrances, the Joint Admission Board runs its own advanced
exam, the Bar Council of India conducts the law entrance, and many states and universities publish
their own notices. Whatever the exam, the document you want is the one on **that body's own site**,
usually called an *information bulletin*, *information sheet* or *notification PDF*.

Aggregator pages are not useless — they are good at telling you the exam exists and when the window
opened. But an aggregator has an incentive to be confident and a deadline to meet, and it is one
step removed from the source. Read its summary, then open the bulletin. If the two disagree, the
bulletin is the fact and the summary is the news.

For anything involving money or a seat, this rule does not have exceptions.

## Read it in this order

A bulletin is 60 to 200 pages. You are not reading all of it, and you should not start at the
syllabus.

1. **"Important dates" table.** Note the *last date of application with the last fee window*, not
   the first closing date. Note the correction window and how long it lasts. Then check the
   footnote: many tables list dates "tentative" and the sentence elsewhere says the authority can
   change them.
2. **"Eligibility" — the two clauses that end applications.** The subject requirement (which
   subjects, in which class, at which level) and the minimum marks or percentile. Read the
   *appearance-only* clause carefully: several exams allow a student who has "appeared" in the
   qualifying exam to apply provisionally, and several do not. Whether "improvement of performance"
   candidates are permitted is a separate sentence in the same section and it has changed year to
   year in some exams.
3. **Age limits, if any.** Some exams have upper and lower age limits, some have relaxation for
   specific categories, some had limits removed in a recent year. Never assume this from memory.
4. **Number of attempts and what counts as one.** Sitting for the exam may not be an attempt;
   qualifying may be. This is worth knowing before you spend a chance.
5. **The exam pattern and marking scheme.** How many questions, how long, whether sections are
   timed separately, and what happens to a wrong answer. Negative marking, where it exists, is a
   strategy question before it is a syllabus question.
6. **Syllabus.** Only now. Compare it against your board's textbook list rather than against a
   coaching module.
7. **Document checklist and photo specifications.** Upload rejections are a real cause of lost
   years, and the size, format and background requirements are stated here and nowhere else.
8. **Fee table and payment modes.** Category, gender and region differences are usually in a table;
   so is whether the fee is refundable.
9. **Grievance and "fair" clauses.** The sections on penalties for malpractice, and the sentence
   stating that the authority's decision is final. Read them once, while calm.

## Write down what is actually written

The single most useful habit: keep a one-page note with **a quotation and a page number** for every
fact you are planning around.

- "Physics, Chemistry and Biology required — bulletin, page 7" is usable.
- "Everyone says Biology is needed" is not.

When a family argues — and stream and exam decisions cause real arguments — a quoted page settles
it. When an advisor, an institute or a website tells you something that contradicts your note, you
have a specific thing to ask about instead of a general feeling of confusion.

## The four claims to refuse

Refuse these unless you can point at the current document:

- **"This year the cutoff will be around X."** Cutoffs are published after the fact by the
  admitting body, and a prediction of one is a guess with a number in it.
- **"You need a particular coaching institute to be eligible."** Eligibility is a rule about
  subjects, marks and age. Anything else is marketing.
- **"The date is definitely this."** Dates move. The bulletin will usually say so itself.
- **"Last year's rules apply."** Read the current one. It takes twenty minutes.
