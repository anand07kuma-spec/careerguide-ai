Most parents in India are involved in their child's career decision. Not always informed, not
always helpful, but involved — and the ones who try hardest are often the ones who accidentally make
it worse. This is a guide to the part a parent can actually control.

## The decision is not yours to make, and that is not the same as being absent

A child choosing a stream at fifteen is choosing under two pressures they did not pick: what they
feel, and what the household will pay for. A parent's real job is the second one — to make sure the
choice is made with the costs, the risks and the fallback visible. Not to pick.

The distinction matters because children experience "advice" as a verdict. Once a parent has stated
the answer, everything after that is the child either complying or hiding things. Both are worse
sources of information than an unanswered question.

## Gather information together, not about them

A parent who researches a course on their own and arrives with conclusions tends to start an
argument. A parent who researches beside the child tends to end up agreeing on facts they did not
expect to.

Concretely, this means:

- **Open the official document together.** One screen, the actual bulletin or the university's
  admissions page. Whoever reads it out loud, whoever writes down the page number. This converts a
  disagreement about the future into a shared task about the present.
- **Ask what the child already believes, before correcting it.** "Who told you that?" and "what did
  they say exactly?" surface the coaching brochure, the cousin, the WhatsApp group. You cannot
  examine a source you have not heard named.
- **Separate the fact from the fear.** "Science is the only safe option" is a fear. "This course
  requires Mathematics in class 12" is a fact. Write them in two columns; the exercise usually
  shrinks the first one.

## The questions that do more work than "what do you want to be"

Fifteen-year-olds cannot answer that, and the asking teaches them that adults are interested in a
label rather than in them. These tend to get real answers:

- "Which class this year did you not mind doing the homework for?"
- "When you explain something to a friend, do you enjoy the explaining or the subject?"
- "Would you rather fix one thing carefully all day, or talk to ten different people?"
- "What would you try for six months if nobody could tell you it was a waste?"
- "Who do you know who does this work — and would you ask them a question if I arranged it?"

Listen for the shape of the answer, not the job title in it. A student who describes loving the
explaining and hating the paperwork is telling you something about environments, and environments are
what determine whether a career lasts.

## Money is a topic, not a verdict

Fees, coaching costs, a city to move to, a sibling's school year — a family that will not discuss
money makes the child guess it, and a fifteen-year-old guessing at costs usually guesses wrong in
the direction of guilt.

Say the actual constraint, once, in plain terms: "This is what we can spend in the next three years,
this is what we would have to give up to spend more." Then let the decision happen inside a
boundaries conversation rather than a secret one. A child who knows the ceiling stops asking for
things above it and starts asking better questions about what is under it.

Equally: do not make an expensive route a moral claim. "We are sacrificing everything for your
coaching" converts a financial decision into a debt the child cannot repay, and it is one of the
most reliably reported sources of pressure on students in this country.

## What not to do

- **Do not compare in public or in the family group.** A cousin's marks are not a route.
- **Do not treat an interest test as a verdict.** A score is a self-description taken at one moment.
  It can widen a list of options; it cannot close one. See [what an interest test can and cannot
  tell you](/blog/riasec-test-explained).
- **Do not rely on a chatbot, a YouTube video or an agent for anything about eligibility, fees,
  seats or deadlines.** Those change, and the people summarising them are often paid per
  enrolment. Use the conducting body's own document — [how to read one](/blog/how-to-read-an-entrance-exam-notification).
- **Do not interrogate a conversation.** If a child is exploring something with a tool or a
  counsellor, asking "what did it say about you?" works better than reading over their shoulder. The
  first keeps them coming back with the second half.

## Staying useful after the decision

The plan will change. A student who takes Science and later moves to Design is not a failed
prediction; that is what the exploration was for. The parent whose approval is attached to the
original answer becomes a reason to hide the change.

Practical ways to keep the route open:

- Know the **lateral entries** for whatever was chosen. Most Indian programmes have transfer routes,
  and they are easier to use if someone in the family already knows they exist.
- Keep a folder — real or digital — of every official document, form receipt and marksheet from the
  first application onward. Verification requests come late and fast.
- Budget time for one **conversation with someone who does the work**, per year. A thirty-minute
  call with a nurse, a CA articleship student or a civil engineer gives a child more than any
  careers fair.
- Watch for the ordinary signs of strain. A career decision that stops being about careers and
  becomes about sleep, appetite or not wanting to attend school is a different problem, and it needs
  a doctor or a counsellor rather than more information. No app, including this one, is that.

A tool can widen a list, explain a route, and say what it does not know. The part that matters most
— a child believing that the decision is survivable either way — is a parent's to give.
