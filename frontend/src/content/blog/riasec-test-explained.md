Ask around for career guidance in India and someone will eventually hand you a test. Usually it is
a multiple-choice questionnaire that ends with a three-letter code and a list of jobs. That code
almost always comes from **RIASEC**, so it is worth knowing what it measures, what it is good for,
and where it stops being useful.

## The six themes

RIASEC is a set of six broad interest areas. The idea comes from the American psychologist John
Holland, who proposed in the 1950s and 60s that people and work environments fall into roughly six
types, and that satisfaction is higher when a person's type matches the environment they are in.

- **Realistic** — working with hands, tools, machines, outdoor setups, physical things. Preference
  for producing a tangible result over discussing an abstract one.
- **Investigative** — observing, questioning, analysing, solving puzzles. Likes figuring out why
  something happens even when there is nothing to sell or no one to persuade.
- **Artistic** — making, arranging, expressing. Drawn to original work, ambiguity and design
  decisions rather than a single correct answer.
- **Social** — teaching, healing, counselling, informing. Gains energy from helping a specific
  person understand or improve something.
- **Enterprising** — persuading, leading, starting, negotiating. Comfortable with risk and speaking
  for a group; likes being responsible for an outcome.
- **Conventional** — organising, recording, ordering, checking. Enjoys accuracy, structure, and
  knowing where the rules come from.

A typical inventory gives you a score on each, and the top two or three become your "code". Someone
who scores high on Investigative, Social and Realistic might be pointed at medicine, laboratory
work, or teaching. That is the whole mechanism.

## What this is genuinely useful for

Interest inventories are not useless; they are **vocabulary**. Most students cannot name more than
ten careers, so a suggestion that "you seem to like fixing things and explaining things" opens two
doors that were not on the list an hour before. The value is in the widening, not in the answer.

They are also good at starting an argument. A result you disagree with is informative: "I got
Enterprising and I definitely do not want to sell anything" is a real signal about the difference
between being good at something and wanting it.

Used well, the output is a **shortlist to go and look at**, ideally next to a description of what
the job is like on an ordinary day. That is exactly why this project pairs its interest themes with
[career profiles](/careers) rather than ending at a score.

## What it cannot do

This is the part most guides skip.

**It cannot measure ability.** An interest inventory asks what you like. It is not a test of
mathematics, or writing, or surgery. Liking physics is not evidence that you will be good at it.

**It cannot be scored from how you speak.** Nothing here can be read from a voice, an accent, a
speaking speed, a facial expression or how confidently a sentence is delivered. Any tool that
claims to infer a child's ability or personality from those is not doing psychology; it is doing
pattern-matching on the wrong signal. This is one reason our guide does not score the way a
conversation sounds.

**It cannot be a diagnosis.** RIASEC is not a clinical instrument. It does not identify a learning
disability, a mental health condition, or intelligence, and it must not be used to explain why a
student struggles. Struggling is a thing to raise with a teacher, a doctor or a specialist who can
actually assess it.

**It is a snapshot of a self-report.** Answers depend on what a student has been told they *should*
like. A fifteen-year-old who has heard that "Science students are serious" may answer to fit. A
score taken at fifteen and treated as a life sentence is the most common way this tool is misused.

**Its match is descriptive, not predictive.** Holland's theory describes how job titles cluster in
surveys of adults. It is weaker at predicting an individual's satisfaction twenty years later, and
career paths in India are now less linear than the theory assumed — a lot of working adults would
not score into the type of the job they hold.

## Reading a result well

If you take one, do this:

1. Look at the **gap between your highest and second-highest**, not the top theme alone. A code
   describes a combination; "Investigative and Enterprising" is a different shape of work from
   "Investigative and Conventional", even though both like analysis.
2. Read the **lowest** theme too. Being low on Conventional tells you something real about the
   working environments that will irritate you.
3. Treat every job suggestion as a **question**. For each one, find out what someone in it does
   between 10am and 1pm. If that description does not appeal, the score did not help you — the
   description did.
4. Retake it after you have tried something new. An inventory taken after a project, an internship
   or a week of shadowing is built on more evidence than one taken before.
